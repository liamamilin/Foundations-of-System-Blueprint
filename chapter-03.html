6  The Knowledge Gap in Software Engineering

Software engineering is often described as a rapidly evolving discipline, characterized by constant innovation in tools, languages, frameworks, and methodologies. New technologies appear each year, and an enormous amount of technical knowledge is shared through conferences, documentation, online forums, blog posts, and open-source projects.

At first glance, the industry appears to have no shortage of knowledge or learning resources.

Yet when engineers attempt to design large, complex systems, a recurring difficulty emerges that cannot be solved simply by accessing more information. While many individual techniques, patterns, and solutions are well understood and widely documented, system-level architectural knowledge remains difficult to reuse effectively.

Teams repeatedly encounter the same fundamental architectural challenges. Decisions about service boundaries, data ownership models, communication patterns, consistency requirements, and operational constraints are often rediscovered and re-solved from scratch rather than reused from prior designs.

This persistent problem suggests that the issue is not the absence of knowledge itself—there is abundant technical knowledge available in books, articles, open-source projects, and online resources. Instead, the difficulty lies in how that knowledge is represented, organized, and transmitted across projects, teams, and organizations.

The gap in software engineering is therefore not primarily a knowledge deficit. It is a representation deficit—the lack of appropriate structures for capturing, organizing, and reusing system architectural knowledge.

6.1 The Problem Is Not Knowledge, but the Form of Knowledge

The software industry has accumulated an extraordinary and valuable amount of technical expertise over decades of practice.

There are well-documented solutions and best practices for numerous technical tasks and problems: database replication strategies, distributed messaging patterns, API design principles, container orchestration approaches, infrastructure automation techniques, and many others. Entire ecosystems exist around these technologies, supported by comprehensive tutorials, detailed documentation, vendor resources, and active community discussions.

However, most of this knowledge exists in forms that are optimized for local problem solving—addressing specific technical questions or implementation challenges—rather than for system-level architectural reasoning—understanding how complete systems are structured and how their components relate to achieve architectural coherence.

Technical articles and tutorials typically describe how to implement a particular feature, solve a specific technical problem, or use a particular library or framework effectively. Documentation explains how to use a specific technology, library, or service. Code repositories demonstrate how a particular project or component was implemented in a specific context.

These resources are valuable and necessary, but they rarely capture the structural logic of an entire system—the coherent framework that explains how components are organized, how they interact through defined interfaces, how data flows across boundaries, how workflows coordinate behavior, and how constraints shape the system’s architecture and evolution.

System architecture involves relationships between many components, interdependencies that create emergent behavior, constraints imposed by operational environments and requirements, and trade-offs between competing design goals. Representing such relationships and architectural coherence requires more than isolated instructions, code examples, or descriptions of individual techniques.

It requires a structured model of the system itself—a representation that captures architectural relationships, component boundaries, interaction patterns, and design rationale in a coherent, analyzable form.

Without such a model, architectural knowledge remains fragmented across many individual explanations, code repositories, and documentation sources, making it difficult to retrieve, synthesize, and reuse when designing new systems.

6.2 Why Stack Overflow and GitHub Cannot Solve System Design Reuse

Online platforms such as Stack Overflow and GitHub have dramatically improved the accessibility and availability of technical knowledge, making it possible for engineers worldwide to learn from each other’s experiences and solutions.

Stack Overflow provides answers to specific, well-defined technical questions, often with detailed explanations and code examples. GitHub offers access to millions of code repositories representing real-world systems, libraries, frameworks, and applications, along with documentation, issues, and discussion threads. Together, these platforms form one of the largest collective knowledge bases in the history of engineering.

However, their strengths also define their fundamental limitations when it comes to system architectural knowledge reuse.

Stack Overflow excels at solving localized, specific problems. Questions typically focus on precise, bounded issues: a configuration error, a programming bug, a library usage problem, or a specific implementation challenge. The answers are optimized for immediate resolution of the specific problem rather than for architectural understanding or systematic reuse of architectural patterns.

GitHub provides access to complete implementations of real systems, but the structural architecture of those implementations is rarely explained or documented in a systematic, analyzable way. While repositories may contain some documentation, architecture diagrams, or design documents, the architectural knowledge behind the system—why components are organized the way they are, what design principles guided the architecture, what trade-offs were made—often remains implicit within the codebase and the minds of the original developers.

As a result, engineers can learn how individual components function, understand specific implementation techniques, and see examples of particular patterns in use. However, they must reconstruct the complete system architecture themselves through code inspection, experimentation, and inference—a process that is time-consuming, incomplete, and prone to misinterpretation.

In practice, this reconstruction process is rarely performed in full or with the rigor necessary for reliable architectural understanding. Developers instead extract fragments of knowledge that are directly relevant to their immediate tasks—the specific technique or pattern they need right now—without developing a comprehensive understanding of the complete architectural context.

The industry therefore benefits from a large, accessible repository of implementation knowledge—how to implement specific features, use particular technologies, and solve specific technical problems—but lacks an equally effective repository of architectural knowledge—how systems are structured as coherent wholes, how components are organized and related, and what architectural patterns produce effective, maintainable systems.

6.3 The Tacit Nature of Architectural Knowledge

One reason architectural knowledge is particularly difficult to reuse is that much of it exists in tacit form—internalized understanding that practitioners possess but rarely articulate explicitly or document systematically.

Tacit knowledge refers to knowledge that emerges through experience, practice, and immersion in a domain rather than through formal instruction or explicit description. It is the knowledge of “how to do” something that experienced practitioners have internalized through repeated application and refinement.

Senior engineers often develop strong intuitions about system design and architecture. They understand how to separate responsibilities across services effectively, how to design stable and maintainable interfaces, how to anticipate operational constraints and performance requirements, and how to balance competing architectural goals.

However, this expertise is typically expressed through practice and action—through the systems they design and build—rather than through formal representation or explicit documentation. The architectural reasoning, design principles, and structural patterns that guide their decisions often remain unarticulated and implicit.

When experienced engineers design systems, they apply internal mental models that have been shaped by years of experience, observation of successful and unsuccessful architectures, and continuous learning from both their own projects and those of others. These mental models guide decisions about architecture—component decomposition, interface design, data ownership, communication patterns—but they are rarely documented in a structured, systematic way that can be communicated effectively to others.

As a result, architectural expertise becomes difficult to transfer from experienced engineers to less experienced team members, from one project to another, or from one organization to another.

Organizations often rely on a small group of individuals who understand the system’s architecture deeply and can make effective architectural decisions. When these individuals leave the organization, move to different roles, or are unavailable, a significant portion of the architectural reasoning and design rationale behind the system may disappear with them.

The architectural knowledge was never truly externalized in a form that could survive beyond the individuals who possessed it.

6.4 Making Implicit Structure Explicit

If architectural knowledge remains primarily tacit and implicit, it cannot accumulate, be systematically communicated, or effectively reused across projects, teams, and organizations.

For software engineering to mature as a discipline capable of building increasingly complex systems reliably and efficiently, system architectural knowledge must be represented in a form that allows it to be captured, communicated, analyzed, and reused effectively.

This requires transforming implicit architectural reasoning and internalized design expertise into explicit structural models that can be documented, shared, and analyzed.

Instead of describing systems only through code or informal, unstructured documentation, engineers must be able to represent the essential structural architecture of a system in an explicit, analyzable form: its components and their responsibilities, interfaces and their contracts, data relationships and ownership boundaries, workflows and their coordination mechanisms, and constraints and their architectural implications.

Such representations allow architectural knowledge to be stored independently of any specific implementation, technology stack, or programming language. The architecture can be understood, analyzed, and discussed at a structural level without being tied to implementation details that may change over time.

Once captured in this form, system structures can be compared to identify similarities and differences, reused as templates or starting points for new systems, and composed into larger architectures through systematic combination rather than ad hoc integration.

Architectural decisions can be examined systematically rather than reconstructed from memory or inferred from code. Design rationale can be preserved alongside structural descriptions, ensuring that future architects understand not only what was built but also why it was built that way.

This book proposes that system blueprints provide the representation framework required to achieve this transformation from implicit, person-dependent architectural knowledge to explicit, reusable structural models.

A blueprint captures the structural essence of a system’s architecture in a form that is both human-readable and machine-usable. By introducing a minimal yet expressive model for describing systems in terms of their architectural components, relationships, and constraints, blueprints make it possible to externalize architectural knowledge and represent it explicitly rather than leaving it implicit and inaccessible.

In doing so, they address the knowledge gap that currently exists between system intent and system implementation—the gap that forces teams to repeatedly rediscover architectural patterns and redesign systems from scratch rather than building upon proven archaeological knowledge.

The next part of this book introduces the fundamental definitions and conceptual framework required to establish this blueprint-based approach to representing and reusing system architectural knowledge.

6.5 Summary

6.5.1 Knowledge Summary

The following concepts are the core of this chapter — you must understand and remember them:

  1. The problem is a representation deficit, not a knowledge deficit: The software industry has abundant technical knowledge. The real gap is the lack of appropriate structures for capturing, organizing, and reusing system-level architectural knowledge. We have plenty of what — we lack the form.

  2. Stack Overflow and GitHub solve implementation knowledge, not architectural knowledge: These platforms excel at localized problem-solving — configuration errors, library usage, specific bugs. They cannot represent how a complete system is structured as a coherent whole. Architectural knowledge remains implicit inside codebases and individual minds.

  3. Most architectural knowledge is tacit: Senior engineers possess deep architectural intuition — component decomposition, interface design, data ownership — but this expertise is rarely externalized in a structured form. When they leave, the architectural reasoning departs with them. Tacit knowledge cannot scale.

  4. The solution is making implicit structure explicit: Architectural knowledge must be transformed from tacit, person-dependent understanding into explicit structural models — blueprints — that can be documented, shared, and analyzed independently of any specific implementation, technology stack, or programming language.
